Chapter 1. Before the Word: A History of the Family

Prologue: A Grave in Eulau

About four and a half thousand years ago, in what is now central Germany, a man, a woman and two boys were laid in the ground together. The older boy was eight or nine, the younger four or five. They had died violently: the bones from the four graves found at the site carry the marks of stone axes, and a flint arrowhead was still lodged in one woman’s spine. Whoever buried them did not do it in a hurry. The dead were arranged with care, their faces turned toward one another, as if those left behind wanted them to stay looking at each other.

When archaeologists excavated the site at Eulau in 2005, that tenderness raised a question no earlier generation could have answered: were these people actually related? Ancient DNA gave the answer three years later. The man and the woman were the father and mother of the two boys. This is the oldest genetically confirmed grave of a mother, a father and their children.1

The same study held two quieter surprises. Chemical traces in the teeth showed that the women had grown up somewhere other than the men and children, so wives had come from outside the community. And in a neighboring grave, children were buried beside a woman who was not their mother: perhaps an aunt, a stepmother, or someone we have no word for. The very first family that science can name already shows the two themes of this chapter. The family is old and deeply felt. And it has never been only one thing.

The family was not invented once. It was assembled from biological parts, shaped by how people earned a living, and fixed in place by law and faith.

1. A Word That Once Meant “Servants”

Begin with the word itself. “Family” comes from the Latin familia, which comes from famulus: a household servant. To a Roman, the familia was not mainly a circle of love. It was a unit of property and authority: everyone and everything under the power of the head of the house, including the slaves.2 Our warmest word for belonging began as a word for ownership.

That should make us careful. When we ask “when did the family begin?”, we are already carrying assumptions: a mother, a father, their children, one roof, shared affection. Scholars have spent a century and a half arguing over whether that picture is universal. In 1949, the anthropologist George Murdock surveyed about 250 societies and concluded that the nuclear family was found everywhere, either on its own or as the building block of larger groups.3 A generation later, feminist anthropologists asked a pointed question in the title of their essay: “Is There a Family?” Their answer was that “the family” is a Western idea projected onto everyone else.4 Today many anthropologists treat kinship as something people do rather than something they are born into: a “mutuality of being” created by feeding, caring, living and grieving together.5

This chapter takes a broad working definition: a family is a socially recognized network of people bound by descent, union or care, organized around raising the next generation. It is wide enough to include a mother nursing her infant two million years ago and a “chosen family” in a modern city. And it lets us ask the real question: not whether humans have families, but how the many forms of family came to be.

2. Milk in the Teeth: The Oldest Bond

The story starts long before words, law or even our own species. It starts with milk.

Teeth grow in daily layers, like tree rings, and they record the chemistry of what a child ate. In 2019, researchers measured barium (an element concentrated in breast milk) in the teeth of Australopithecus africanus, an ancestor who lived in southern Africa more than two million years ago. The pattern was clear: intensive nursing for roughly the first year, then weaning, and then, again and again over several years, a return to breast milk whenever food was scarce.6 A hungry child went back to its mother.

We should not overstate this. A long bond between mother and infant is a mammal trait; chimpanzee mothers have it too. Those teeth do not prove a “family”. But they show the foundation everything else was built on: a child who depends on someone else for years, and an adult who keeps responding. The rest of this story is about who else came to help.

3. It Takes More Than a Mother

Human children are expensive. They are born helpless, grow slowly, and need food for well over a decade before they can feed themselves. And human mothers, unlike chimpanzee mothers, often have a new baby before the previous child is independent. On her own, no mother could manage this. So who helped?

One answer came from studies among the Hadza, foragers in Tanzania. The anthropologist Kristen Hawkes and her colleagues noticed that older women worked hard, digging tubers and bringing food to their grandchildren. Their “grandmother hypothesis” proposes that women who lived past their own childbearing years could keep their daughters’ children fed, which let those daughters wean earlier and have more children. Natural selection would then favor longer lives, and humans are indeed one of the very few species in which females routinely live decades past menopause.7

The primatologist Sarah Blaffer Hrdy took the idea further. In Mothers and Others she argued that humans evolved as “cooperative breeders”: children were raised not by mothers alone but by mothers and others, including grandmothers, older siblings, aunts, fathers and neighbors. A baby that could read faces and win over many caretakers had a better chance to survive, and that pressure, she suggests, helped shape the human talent for understanding other minds.8 Not everyone accepts the label: some biologists argue that humans are not cooperative breeders in the strict sense used for meerkats and marmosets, and prefer the term “biocultural reproduction”, in which help is organized through culture and kinship.9 But the core point is hardly disputed: the mother-and-father-alone household is not the human default. Raising children together is.

4. The Bond of Two

Then there is the pair bond: two adults committed to each other over time. Gorillas live in harems, chimpanzees in groups with many males, bonobos in famously relaxed arrangements. Humans, across most cultures, form long-term couples recognized by their community. When and why this began is among the most debated questions in human evolution.

Several explanations compete. The oldest says males provisioned females and offspring, and females favored reliable providers.10 Others look across all mammals and propose that monogamy evolves where females are too spread out for males to guard several, or where males need to protect infants from rival males.11 A newer model ties pair bonding back to grandmothers: as people lived longer, older men who could still father children outnumbered fertile women, so men did better by guarding one partner.12 None of these is settled. What is clear is that, at some point in our lineage, the couple was added to the mother-and-child core.

The primatologist Bernard Chapais argues that this addition was the decisive moment. A pair bond did something no other primate arrangement could do: it made fathers recognizable, and it created in-laws. Once a daughter married into another group, two groups were tied together through her. In Chapais’s account, human society itself, with its webs of alliances across communities, grew out of marriage.13

5. Bands of Strangers

For about 95 percent of our species’ history, humans lived by foraging. We cannot visit those bands, but we can study the hunter-gatherer societies that survived into the modern era. When anthropologists finally counted who actually lived with whom, the result overturned an old picture.

The old picture showed tight bands of brothers, their wives and children: a clan of blood relatives. In 2011, Kim Hill and colleagues analyzed residence in 32 foraging societies covering more than five thousand people. Most people in a band were not close genetic relatives. Men and women both moved in and out of groups. Adult brothers and sisters often lived together, and in-laws were everywhere.14 Other studies confirmed that after marriage, couples moved flexibly, sometimes living with the wife’s family, sometimes the husband’s, sometimes moving between them.15 One elegant model found that if women and men have an equal say in where a couple lives, this is exactly the pattern that emerges, even when everyone would prefer to live near their own relatives.16

The ancient bones agree. At Sunghir, in Russia, people were buried with thousands of ivory beads around 34,000 years ago. Their genomes show that the individuals were not closely related, and that their population avoided inbreeding much as modern foragers do: through mating networks that reached well beyond the home band.17 We cannot say whether anyone knew that marrying a close relative carries risks. Something else may have done the work. As Edvard Westermarck proposed in 1891, children raised together tend to lose sexual interest in each other, an effect strikingly confirmed by studies of Taiwanese couples who were raised as children in the same household.18

Marriage itself seems to have deep roots. By reconstructing the history of marriage customs across hunter-gatherer language families, researchers concluded that marriages arranged by relatives, and payments in labor or goods to the bride’s family, are probably very ancient, possibly as old as the spread of modern humans out of Africa.19 Families at this stage were not closed boxes. Food was shared across the whole band. Men and women divided much of the work, though less rigidly than the “Man the Hunter” stereotype suggests: a 2023 survey found women hunting in most of the foraging societies it examined, although its methods remain debated.20 The forager family was porous, mobile and woven into a larger web. If there was a “first family”, it looked less like a house and more like a net.

6. Seeds, Walls and Inheritance

About twelve thousand years ago, in the hills of Southwest Asia, some people began to stay put. They planted wheat and barley, kept goats and sheep, and built houses of mud brick. Farming would emerge independently in several other parts of the world. Nothing changed the family more.

Foragers own little and move often. Farmers own fields, houses, stored grain and herds, and these things outlast the people who own them. For the first time it really mattered who your father was, which son would inherit the field, and which family a daughter’s labor and children would belong to. Across the world’s societies, a dependence on farming goes with “tighter” kinship: clans, lineages, cousin marriage, and strict rules about where a married couple lives.21 Herding and plough agriculture often pushed societies toward descent through the male line. In Africa, the spread of cattle herding is linked to the loss of matrilineal descent. But elsewhere, in parts of the Pacific for example, residence with the wife’s family was probably the older pattern.22

Nowhere is the logic of property and lineage clearer than among cattle herders. Among the Nuer of South Sudan, studied by the anthropologist E. E. Evans-Pritchard in the 1930s, a marriage was made by moving cattle: the groom’s kin handed a herd to the bride’s kin, and with it the children she would bear came to belong to the groom’s lineage. Who had paid mattered more than who was the biological father. If a man died without sons, a kinsman could marry a wife “to his name”, and her children were counted as the dead man’s: a “ghost marriage”. A wealthy, childless woman could even pay bridewealth for a wife of her own and be recognized as the “father” of that wife’s children.23 To an outsider these arrangements look strange. To the Nuer they made perfect sense, because a family was a line of descent to be kept alive, not a couple to be made happy.

The earliest farming villages already show the family changing shape. At Aşıklı Höyük and Boncuklu in central Turkey, in the 9th and 8th millennia BCE, people buried their dead beneath the floors of their homes. Ancient DNA shows that those buried in the same house were often siblings, or parents and their children: the first direct genetic sign of close relatives sharing a household. But at the larger and later town of Çatalhöyük, the children buried together in a house were mostly unrelated.24 Already, ten thousand years ago, “household” did not always mean “blood”.

7. Family Trees Written in Bone

For most of history, the family trees of ordinary people were lost the moment their grandchildren forgot them. Ancient DNA has changed that. In the last few years, geneticists have rebuilt real family trees, generation by generation, from the bones of people who never wrote anything down.

At Hazleton North, a stone tomb in the English Cotswolds built about 5,700 years ago, researchers identified a family spanning five generations. One man had children with four different women, and each woman’s descendants were buried in their own section of the tomb. Every link from one generation to the next ran through men. Women who married in were buried there; daughters who married out were not. The tomb also held stepsons, men whose mothers had children with a man of the lineage, and several people who were not related at all. A Neolithic family, it turns out, could include the “blended” and the adopted.25

At Gurgy in France, about 6,700 years ago, a cemetery of 94 sequenced individuals produced two family trees covering seven generations, again organized through fathers, with wives arriving from neighboring communities. Strikingly, there were almost no half-siblings, which suggests unions that lasted.26 In southern Germany’s Lech valley, Bronze Age farmsteads held a high-status core family across generations, buried alongside unrelated people of lower status who were perhaps servants or dependents: a prehistoric version of the Roman familia.27

Then the picture turns. At Fujia in eastern China, around 2750–2500 BCE, two cemeteries were organized strictly by maternal clans, with a matrilineal line lasting at least ten generations.28 At Mokrin in Serbia, Bronze Age women could inherit status but not pass it to all their sons.29 The lesson is not that our ancestors were patriarchal, or that they were matriarchal. The lesson is that there was never one original family. There were many, shaped by land, wealth and custom, and the patrilineal pattern that dominates the European samples may partly reflect which burials archaeologists happened to dig: monumental tombs, which tend to belong to powerful lineages.

8. When the Family Became Law

Writing was invented to count grain, but it soon began to record marriages. The first states did something no forager band had done: they wrote the family down, and in doing so they made it something rulers could command.

The oldest law code we have, from Ur-Nammu of Ur in about 2100 BCE, already sets the silver a man must pay if he divorces his first-ranking wife. Three centuries later, the famous stele of Hammurabi of Babylon devotes dozens of its roughly 280 laws to the household: betrothal, bride-wealth and dowry, adultery, divorce, adoption, inheritance and the care of widows.30 Its logic is clear. The groom’s family paid a bride-wealth (terhatum) to the bride’s father. The bride brought a dowry (šeriktum) that remained hers and passed to her children. A wife whose husband neglected her could, in some cases, leave and take her dowry with her. A son who struck his father would lose his hand. The household (bītum, “house”) was an economic firm, a religious unit and a small kingdom ruled by its father. The family was also expected to continue after death: children owed their dead parents regular offerings, and to die without descendants was to go hungry in the afterlife.

Even here, the law and real life diverged. In the city of Sippar, women called nadītum lived unmarried in a cloister, owned property and lent money. In the trading city of Assur, merchants’ wives ran businesses while their husbands traveled.31 The historian Gerda Lerner argued that Mesopotamia is where patriarchy was created: not as a fact of nature but as a historical system in which women’s sexuality and reproduction became property regulated by men and the state.32 Her thesis remains debated in its details, but it changed how historians read these laws.

In Egypt, women had legal standing unusual for the ancient world: they could own and inherit property, sign contracts, go to court and initiate divorce.33 Around 1145 BCE, in the village of the royal tomb-builders at Deir el-Medina, a woman named Naunakhte made a will in front of a court. She was old and listed which of her children had cared for her, and she disinherited those who had not.34 The scene would be familiar in any modern probate court. Marriage in Egypt needed no priest; it was a private agreement, often secured by a property contract. Religion presented its ideal family in myth: Osiris, his wife Isis and their son Horus, the faithful wife who rebuilt her murdered husband and the son who avenged his father.

9. Households of the Classical World

In classical Athens, the household (oikos) was a unit of people, land, slaves and ancestral worship, and its continuity mattered more than anyone’s happiness. A woman remained under the legal guardianship of a male kyrios all her life. If a man died leaving only a daughter, she could be required to marry a close male relative so the estate stayed in the family.35 Sparta did things very differently, which is a useful reminder that “the Greek family” was never one thing.

Rome took paternal power furthest. The paterfamilias, the oldest living male, held patria potestas over his descendants for as long as he lived, in theory including the power of life and death. The Twelve Tables of about 450 BCE even stated that a father who sold his son into bondage three times lost his power over him, which tells us such sales happened.36 But the historian Richard Saller showed that demographics softened the law: with short life expectancy, many Romans lost their fathers before adulthood, and the all-powerful elderly patriarch was more legal theory than common reality.37 By the late Republic most wives married without passing into their husbands’ power, which gave elite women real control of property. Then came the first great experiment in state family policy: in 18 BCE and 9 CE, the emperor Augustus passed laws that penalized unmarried and childless members of the elite and rewarded parents of three or more children.38 Rulers worried about the birth rate two thousand years before modern governments did.

Rome also shows how flexible “blood” could be when property was at stake. Elite Romans routinely adopted adult men to carry on a family name and estate. The most famous case changed world history: in his will of 44 BCE, Julius Caesar adopted his great-nephew Octavian, who became Caesar’s son in law and later ruled as Augustus.39 For Rome, a son was someone the familia needed, and the law could create one.

10. Ancestors and Elders: China and South Asia

In China, Confucian thought made the family the model of the whole political order. Filial piety (xiao) bound children to parents, and the subject’s duty to the ruler mirrored the son’s duty to the father. Ancestor worship joined the living to the dead, and over the centuries large lineages sharing a surname, land and ancestral halls became powerful social institutions, especially in southern China. Historians such as Patricia Ebrey have shown how much room women nonetheless had inside this structure, negotiating dowries and running households.40 And the bones of Fujia remind us that China’s deeper past was not always organized through the father.

In South Asia, the ideal of the joint family, in which brothers, their wives and children share a home and property under the eldest male, took root and still endures. Texts such as the Manusmṛti set out the duties of the householder (gṛhastha), the stage of life on which all others were said to depend, and rules forbidding marriage within one’s own lineage (gotra). Marriage was understood as a sacred duty binding families and generations, not only two people.41 But such texts tell us what their authors thought people should do, not always what they did, and the same caution applies to every ancient law code in this section.

Across the first civilizations, three ideas recur: the household as a unit of economy, law and worship; authority held by a senior man; and marriage as a contract between families about property and heirs.

11. Contract and Scripture: The Islamic Family

In the 7th century CE a new body of family law emerged in Arabia, and within a few generations it governed households from Spain to Central Asia. Islamic law treated marriage (nikāḥ) as a contract, not a sacrament. Its central payment, the mahr, was owed by the groom to the bride herself, not to her father, and it remained her own property. The Qur’an permitted a man up to four wives, but only on the condition that he treat them justly, and it gave daughters a fixed share of inheritance, half that of a son: unequal by modern standards, but a guaranteed share at a time when many legal systems gave daughters nothing.42

As with every legal tradition in this chapter, the text was only the beginning. Jurists built up elaborate rules on custody, maintenance and divorce. A husband could divorce by repudiation (ṭalāq), while a wife could seek release by returning her mahr (khulʿ) or petition a judge. Historians who read actual court records, from Ottoman Anatolia to Mamluk Cairo, find women going to court to claim maintenance, property and custody, and judges often granting it.43 Polygyny was legally possible but, for most households, economically out of reach. The ordinary Muslim household, like most households in this story, was one husband, one wife and their children, set within a dense web of relatives.

12. The Hearth Prince: Families of the Steppe

Farming was not the only path to the family. On the grasslands of Inner Asia, herding peoples built a family system around animals, mobility and the felt tent. The Secret History of the Mongols, written in the 13th century, opens a window onto it that no archaeological site can match.

Its story of Chinggis Khan begins as a family drama. When the boy Temüjin was about nine, his father Yesügei took him to find a bride, betrothed him to a girl named Börte, and left him to live with her family: a custom that echoes the bride-service of far older foragers. On the way home Yesügei was poisoned. His followers abandoned the widow Hö’elün and her children, and she kept them alive by digging roots and catching fish along the Onon River. Years later, Börte was abducted by a rival clan, and Temüjin’s campaign to rescue her was the start of his rise.44 The empire that would stretch from Korea to Hungary began with a mother who refused to let her family die and a husband who would not give up his wife.

The rules behind the story are just as revealing. Mongols married outside their own clan (obog). Elder sons received a share of the herds when they married and set up their own tents, while the youngest son, the otchigin or “prince of the hearth”, inherited his father’s home tent and pastures and the duty to care for his mother. A widow might marry her late husband’s younger brother, or his son by another wife, keeping her, her children and her herds within the lineage. Within the tent, women ran the household economy and often the camp itself when men were away at war or with the herds, and widows such as Hö’elün and, later, Sorghaghtani Beki wielded real political power.45 Ancient DNA tells a similar story for earlier steppe peoples: in Avar-period cemeteries in Hungary, large pedigrees trace male lines across generations, with wives coming from outside.46 The steppe family was patrilineal, but it was not a world in which women had no power.

13. The Church and the Shrinking Clan

If one force made the European family unusual, it was the Western Church. From late antiquity onward, Church councils steadily widened the ban on marrying relatives: first cousins, then second cousins, eventually as far as sixth cousins, until the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215 scaled the ban back. The Church also opposed marrying a dead brother’s widow, polygamy, and adoption as a way of passing on inheritance, and it insisted that a valid marriage required the consent of the bride and groom.

The anthropologist Jack Goody argued in 1983 that these rules broke apart the large kin groups that dominated most of the world, and, not by coincidence, left more land without heirs to pass to the Church.47 Historians still debate his motive; the rules themselves are not in doubt. More recently, Joseph Henrich and colleagues proposed that centuries of exposure to these marriage rules help explain why Western populations today are unusually individualistic and trusting of strangers. It is a bold and much-contested claim.48 Meanwhile, marriage became formal and public. In 1563 the Council of Trent required a priest and witnesses for a valid Catholic marriage. England required church weddings with public notice from 1753, and revolutionary France introduced civil marriage, and divorce, in 1792.49 The state and the Church now competed for control of the family.

Ordinary Europeans, meanwhile, developed a remarkable pattern. In 1965 the demographer John Hajnal mapped a line running roughly from St Petersburg to Trieste. West of it, people married late (women typically in their mid-twenties), a large share never married at all, and couples set up their own households, often after years working as servants in someone else’s home.50 Peter Laslett and the Cambridge Group then counted English households across three centuries and found something that should have been impossible under the old story: between 1564 and 1821, only about one household in ten held relatives beyond parents and children. That was the same share as in 1966.51 The small, independent household of northwestern Europe existed long before factories. Some historians even argue that it helped make industrialization possible, rather than the other way around. Eastern and southern Europe and much of Asia, by contrast, kept larger joint and multi-generational households, which are just as “traditional”.

14. Did They Love Their Children?

In 1960 a French writer named Philippe Ariès, who made his living as a documentation specialist at a tropical-fruit institute and called himself a “Sunday historian”, published a book that launched a field. He argued that medieval Europe had no real concept of childhood: children were treated as small adults, and the loving, child-centered family was a modern invention.52 Lawrence Stone and Edward Shorter extended the idea, describing a shift from cold, kin-dominated households toward affectionate nuclear families between 1500 and 1800.53

It was a powerful idea, and it turned out to be largely wrong. When the historian Linda Pollock read hundreds of diaries and letters, she found parents in every century worrying over sick children, grieving the dead and delighting in the living. Medievalists found the same.54 What changed over time was not the existence of love but its expression and its ideals: how childhood was organized, how marriage was justified, and what people expected a family to feel like. Ariès was wrong about the heart, but right that its institutions have a history.

15. Factory and Hearth

Industrialization did not create the nuclear family. Laslett had shown that. What it changed was what the family did. For most of history the household was a workplace: the farm, the workshop, the shop. When work moved into factories and offices, the family became a unit of consumption, child-raising and emotion, and the day was split between “work” and “home”.

That split became an ideology. The Victorian middle class idealized “separate spheres”: the husband as breadwinner in the harsh public world, the wife as moral guardian of a private haven.55 It was mostly a middle-class ideal; working-class women and children kept working for wages. Kinship did not dissolve under these pressures. In the mill town of Preston in 1851, migrants leaned more heavily on relatives for housing and jobs, and in New Hampshire’s Amoskeag mills, whole families recruited and protected one another.56

The law moved slowly. Under English “coverture”, a married woman’s legal identity was absorbed into her husband’s: she could not own property or sign contracts in her own name. Reform came piece by piece: limited custody rights for mothers in 1839, and married women’s property acts in 1870 and 1882.57 In Japan, the Meiji Civil Code of 1898 did the opposite. It turned the ie, the patrilineal household under a male head, into national law, and it was abolished only after 1946, when the new Constitution declared that marriage rests on the mutual consent of both sexes.58 In 1948, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights gave the family its first global charter. Article 16 called the family the “natural and fundamental group unit of society”, entitled to protection, and declared the right of men and women to marry freely and on equal terms.59

Sociologists such as Talcott Parsons concluded that the “isolated nuclear family” was the perfect fit for industrial society, and William Goode predicted that the whole world would converge on it.60 History had other plans.

16. Paper Families: The State Writes Everyone Down

The modern state wanted to count families, and counting changed them. From 1 July 1837, every birth, marriage and death in England and Wales had to be registered with the state, not just the parish.61 In Japan, the Meiji government’s family register law of 1871, implemented in 1872, recorded every subject as a member of a household (koseki) with a named head: a family defined by an official document.62 The koseki survives today, now centered on the married couple, and with it a rule that spouses must share one surname. Japan’s Supreme Court has twice upheld that rule, in 2015 and 2021, and the question of letting couples keep separate names remains one of the country’s liveliest family debates.63

Paper could also create a family. In 1851 Massachusetts passed what is generally regarded as the first modern adoption law, which put the welfare of the child, judged by a court, at the center of adoption rather than the inheritance needs of the adopting family.64 Rome had adopted heirs. Modern law began to adopt children. In 1989 the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child went further, treating children as people with rights of their own, including the right to know and be cared for by their parents and to be heard in decisions about their lives.65

The state could also reach into the bedroom. Between 1980 and 2015, China limited most couples to a single child, the most ambitious attempt in history to engineer family size. It moved to a two-child policy in 2016 and a three-child policy in 2021, as the worry shifted from too many births to too few.66 Augustus would have recognized the problem.

17. The Short Golden Age

The image many people still call “the traditional family” (a married father who goes out to work, a mother at home, two or three children, a house in the suburbs) peaked in the West in the 1950s. It was, as the historian Stephanie Coontz showed, a historical anomaly: marriage ages fell to record lows, marriage rates and birth rates soared after the war, and a single wage could support a household. Just as remarkable, it is the first era in which love, rather than property or alliance, was widely expected to be the whole basis of marriage.67 That ideal turned out to undo the very model it built. If marriage exists for love, then a marriage without love has lost its reason to exist.

18. Unbundling the Family

Since the 1960s, the things the family used to bundle together (sex, marriage, living together, having children, economic support and legal recognition) have come apart. Demographers call it the “Second Demographic Transition”: later marriage, more cohabitation, more divorce, more children born outside marriage, and fertility below the level needed to replace each generation.68 In 1967, the US Supreme Court struck down bans on interracial marriage. In 1978, Louise Brown, the first child conceived through IVF, was born in England, and conception was separated from intercourse.69 No-fault divorce spread across the West. In 2001 the Netherlands became the first country to open marriage to same-sex couples. The United States followed nationwide in 2015, and by 2026 same-sex marriage is legal in 38 countries, most recently Thailand in January 2025.7071 Researchers began writing about “families we choose”: networks of friends who act as kin when biological families reject or are absent.72

Some of the strain is demographic. The world’s fertility rate has fallen from 3.31 births per woman in 1990 to about 2.25 in 2024.73 In Japan, births to Japanese citizens fell to 671,236 in 2025, the lowest since records began in 1899, with a total fertility rate of 1.14.74 Families are getting smaller, older and more “vertical”: more grandparents and fewer cousins. That raises urgent questions that earlier generations answered through kinship: who will care for the old, who will raise the young, and who pays.

19. The Questions Ahead

The family that enters the middle of the 21st century faces problems that no earlier form has had to solve, and old problems in new shapes.

Who will care? For most of history, the old were cared for by their children, and the young by a crowd of relatives. Smaller families and longer lives have broken that equation. A single child may one day be responsible for two parents and four grandparents, while also raising children of their own: the “sandwich generation”. Japan, where more people die each year than are born, is the clearest case, but it is not unusual. Every aging society is now asking what part of care belongs to families, to the market and to the state.

Can people afford a family? Across East Asia especially, young adults say they want to marry and have children but delay or give up because of housing costs, long working hours, insecure jobs and the cost of education. In Japan, marriages rose slightly in 2025, to 489,119, but in Tokyo the fertility rate stayed below one child per woman, at 0.96.75 Where having children outside marriage remains rare, fewer marriages mean fewer births.

Who does the work at home? Women’s paid work has transformed the family. Men’s share of housework and care has risen far more slowly, a gap the sociologist Arlie Hochschild called “the second shift”. Some researchers describe the gender revolution as “stalled”: open doors outside the home, but an unequal division of labor inside it.76

Does the law fit real families? Blended families, cohabiting couples, same-sex parents, single parents, families spread across borders, and children born through donors or surrogacy all live inside legal systems designed for a married man, a married woman and their biological children. Thailand’s marriage equality law, which took effect in January 2025, also set the minimum age of marriage at 18, a reminder that family law reform often moves on several fronts at once.77 But even where same-sex couples can marry, the law of parenthood often lags behind. Research on children in these “new” families suggests that what matters most for a child is the quality of relationships at home, not the shape of the family.78

Who counts as a parent? Reproductive technology has split motherhood into genetic, gestational and social parts, and made it possible for a child to have more than two people with some claim to be a parent. Courts and parliaments are still deciding how to recognize them.

None of these questions is entirely new. Families have always had to answer who cares, who pays, who does the work and who belongs. What is new is that, for the first time, individuals rather than lineages, churches or states are increasingly the ones expected to answer them.

Epilogue: What the Long Story Teaches

Step back and the long story has a shape. It begins with milk in the teeth of a hungry child two million years ago. It grows through grandmothers and helpers, the bond of two adults, and bands of foragers linked by marriage across the landscape. It hardens with farming into lineages and inheritance, gets written into the laws of Babylon and Rome, is reshaped by the Church, taken apart by the factory and reassembled by love, and then, in our own lifetimes, unbundled into forms the Victorians could not have imagined.

Every age has believed that its own family was the natural one, and that the family was in crisis. Augustus did. The Victorians did. The 1950s did, and so do we. But the evidence of two million years points to a different conclusion. The family’s one constant is not its form, which has always varied, but its purpose: to bind people together so that the helpless can grow up, the old are not abandoned, and no one has to face the world alone. The forms will keep changing. The question for our century, and for the chapters that follow, is not whether the family will survive, but which forms will best serve that purpose.

The family has never been one thing. It has always been the human answer to a single problem: how to raise the helpless together.

D1. Core Synthesis: Ten Key Findings

This is the meta-level argument that the evidence supports. Each point is developed in the sections that follow.

  1. The family is older than our species, but not in its current form. [A] Long mother–infant bonds, extended juvenile dependence and shared provisioning came before Homo sapiens. What is distinctively human is how those elements were combined: pair bonds, investment by fathers, grandmothers, and ties between groups formed through marriage.

  2. Humans are "cooperative breeders" (or something close). [B] A child has never been raised by its mother alone, or by its mother and father alone. Grandmothers, siblings and other relatives (called "alloparents") were part of the human design. Whether the technical term "cooperative breeder" fits exactly is debated (Section 3), but the core point is not.

  3. Hunter-gatherer families were flexible, not tribal clans. [A] In 32 foraging societies, most people living in the same band were not genetically related; either sex could stay with or leave their birth group, and adult brothers and sisters often lived together (Hill et al., 2011, Science).

  4. Marriage as a social institution is very old. [B] Phylogenetic reconstruction suggests that arranged marriage and exchange between groups go back deep into the history of Homo sapiens, possibly to the dispersal out of Africa (Walker et al., 2011, PLOS ONE).

  5. Agriculture "tightened" kinship. [B] Land, storage and inheritance favored lineages, clans, households that persisted across generations, and, in many regions, descent through the male line and wives moving to the husband's family.

  6. Ancient DNA shows diversity, not a single template. [A] The European Neolithic and Bronze Age burials studied so far are mostly patrilineal, with wives moving to the husband's community (Eulau, Hazleton North, Gurgy, Lech valley). But early Anatolia shows close kin buried together within houses, and Fujia in China (2750–2500 BCE) was organized around two maternal clans.

  7. Early states wrote the family into law. [A] From the laws of Ur-Nammu (around 2100 BCE) and Hammurabi (18th century BCE) to Roman patria potestas, the household became a legal, economic and religious unit headed, in most cases, by a senior male.

  8. The "Western nuclear family" is old and unusual, and came before industrialization. [B] The Church's bans on marrying relatives (Goody, 1983), the late-marriage pattern west of the Hajnal line, and English household data (Laslett) all point to small households centuries before factories existed.

  9. Industrialization reorganized roles more than it changed household size. [A] The big change was the split between paid work and the home: the male-breadwinner and female-homemaker ideal ("separate spheres"). The idea that industrialization shrank big extended families into nuclear ones is largely a myth.

  10. Today's diversity of family forms is a return to flexibility, not a collapse. [B] Seen across the long history, the mid-20th-century breadwinner family is the historical anomaly (Coontz). Diverse, negotiated, multi-adult care arrangements are closer to the human norm.

The book’s possible thesis line (yours to adapt)

The family was not invented once. It was assembled from biological parts (attachment, pair bonding, grandmothering), shaped by how people made a living (foraging, farming, wages), and fixed in place by law and religion. Every “traditional family” in history was someone’s recent innovation.

D2. What Is “Family”? Definitions and Their Problems

Any history of the family depends on its definition. Scholars use at least four different lenses, and much of the disagreement in the field comes from mixing them up.

Lens Defines family as Key source
Kinship / descent People linked by blood (consanguinity) or marriage (affinity) Morgan (1871); Lévi-Strauss (1949)
Household / co-residence People who live and eat together Laslett & Wall (1972); Hajnal (1982)
Function The unit that handles reproduction, child-rearing, economic cooperation and sexual regulation Murdock (1949); Parsons & Bales (1955)
Relatedness / practice Whoever people treat and feel as kin; family is something people do Schneider (1984); Carsten (2004); Sahlins (2013); Weston (1991)

The universality debate

  • Murdock (1949), Social Structure: surveyed about 250 societies and argued that the nuclear family is universal, either alone or as the building block of larger forms. [C] He was influential, but his conclusion depends heavily on how the categories were defined.

  • The critique: Collier, Rosaldo & Yanagisako (1982), "Is There a Family?", and Schneider (1984) argued that "family" is a Western cultural category projected onto other societies. [B]

  • The current middle ground: Some bonds are near-universal: mother–child, a recognized form of marriage or union, and incest avoidance. But which bonds count as family, who lives together, and who holds authority vary enormously. [A]

Writing tip

Define “family” at the start of the chapter, and say which lens you are using. A good working definition for a long history: a socially recognized network of people bound by descent, union or care, organized around raising the next generation. This lets you include a 2-million-year-old mother–infant pair and a 21st-century “chosen family” in the same story.

D3. Deep Origins: Biology Before Culture

D3.1 Mother–infant bonds in Australopithecus

Barium levels in the tooth enamel of Australopithecus africanus (South Africa, about 2.6–2.1 million years ago) indicate intensive nursing for roughly the first year, followed by repeated returns to breast milk during times of food shortage, cycling over several years (Joannes-Boyau et al., 2019, Nature). [B]

Interpretive caution: A long mother–infant bond is a mammal and primate trait. It is the raw material of the family, not proof of a family in the human sense. Use it as the "first chapter of attachment", not the "first family".

D3.2 The pair bond

Humans form long-term pair bonds. Most other apes do not. When and why this happened is one of the most debated questions in the field. [C]

Hypothesis Core idea Key source
Provisioning ("sex for food") Males provisioned females and offspring; females preferred reliable providers Lovejoy (1981, Science); Gavrilets (2012, PNAS)
Mate guarding Monogamy evolves where females are spread out and males cannot guard several Lukas & Clutton-Brock (2013, Science)
Infanticide protection Pair living protects infants from infanticidal males (in primates) Opie et al. (2013, PNAS)
Grandmothering → pair bonding Longer lives skewed the sex ratio of fertile adults toward men, so men gained by guarding a partner Coxworth et al. (2015, PNAS)

Smaller size differences between males and females in Homo erectus (from about 1.9 million years ago) are often read as a sign of reduced male–male competition and more pair bonding. That reading is debated. [C]

D3.3 The grandmother hypothesis

Humans live long past menopause; other great apes do not. Hawkes et al. (1998, PNAS), working with Hadza foragers in Tanzania, proposed that grandmothers who gather food for their grandchildren let their daughters wean earlier and have children closer together, so natural selection favored longer life. [B] This is widely cited and supported by models and some demographic data, but alternatives exist (for example, men's hunting, or the "embodied capital" model of Kaplan et al., 2000).

D3.4 Cooperative breeding and alloparenting

Sarah Blaffer Hrdy, Mothers and Others (2009): human mothers evolved to rely on helpers. Shared infant care shaped human social intelligence, including our ability to read other people's intentions. [B]

  • Counterpoint: Bogin, Bragg & Kuzawa (2014) argue that humans are not cooperative breeders in the strict biological sense. They propose "biocultural reproduction" instead: help is organized by culture and kinship, not by breeding hierarchies as in meerkats or marmosets. Present this as an open debate. [C]

  • Timing: The idea that cooperative breeding began with Homo erectus about 1.9 million years ago is an inference from brain size, slow development and energy costs (Aiello & Key, 2002). There is no direct evidence of it.

D3.5 Incest avoidance

Westermarck (1891) proposed that people who grow up together in early childhood develop a sexual aversion to each other. The strongest test is Arthur Wolf's study of Taiwanese "minor marriages", in which a future bride was raised from infancy in her future husband's household. These marriages had higher divorce rates and lower fertility (Wolf, 1995). [A]

Ancient DNA from Sunghir, Russia (about 34,000 years ago) shows that the individuals buried there were not closely related, and their population had low inbreeding, similar to modern hunter-gatherers living within wider mating networks (Sikora et al., 2017, Science). [A] Careful: this shows a pattern of avoiding close relatives as partners. It does not prove that people consciously "knew" inbreeding was harmful.

D4. Hunter-Gatherers: The First Human Family Systems

Humans lived as foragers for about 95% of our species' history. Living hunter-gatherers are not "living fossils", but they are our best model for how families were organized before farming.

D4.1 Who lived together

  • *Hill et al. (2011, Science): 32 foraging societies, 5,067 individuals. Either sex may disperse from or remain in the group they were born into. Adult brothers and sisters often live together. Most people in a band are not genetically related. [A]*

  • *Marlowe (2004, Current Anthropology): residence among foragers is mostly multilocal: a couple may live with the wife's kin, the husband's kin, or move between the two. This contradicts the old "patrilocal band" model. [A]*

  • *Dyble et al. (2015, Science): an agent-based model with data from the Agta (Philippines) and Mbendjele BaYaka (Congo). If men and women have equal say in where a couple lives, relatedness within camps falls, even though everyone prefers to live near kin. [B]*

  • *Apicella et al. (2012, Nature): Hadza social networks show that cooperation clusters among people who choose to live together, not only among kin. [B]*

D4.2 Marriage and the links between groups

Walker et al. (2011, PLOS ONE) reconstructed marriage practices across hunter-gatherer language families. They found that marriages arranged by kin and some form of bride-service (labor or goods given to the bride's family) are likely very ancient, possibly as old as the spread of modern humans out of Africa. [B]

*Chapais, Primeval Kinship (2008): this is the key synthesis. Chapais argues that human society began when pair bonds were added to primate kin groups. Pair bonds allowed fathers to be recognized and in-law ties to form, so that one marriage linked two groups. Marriage was "invented" as an alliance between groups, an idea that echoes Lévi-Strauss. [B]*

D4.3 Roles and values

  • Division of labor existed, but it was flexible and often overstated. Anderson et al. (2023, PLOS ONE) found women hunting in a majority of the foraging societies they surveyed. [B] The methods are contested; present it as challenging, not overturning, the "Man the Hunter" model.

  • Sharing norms (demand sharing, food sharing across the whole band) made the family porous: the household was not an economically closed unit. [A]

  • Relative gender egalitarianism compared with farming societies is well supported in general (Dyble 2015; Hill 2011), though foragers vary widely. [B]

D5. The Neolithic Shift and the Ancient DNA Revolution

D5.1 Why farming changed the family

Farming began in Southwest Asia roughly 12,000–11,000 years ago and arose independently in several other regions. It created what had not existed before: fixed property, stored surplus, inheritable land and a need for labor. Cross-cultural studies link these pressures to "tighter" kinship: lineages, clans, marriage between cousins, and residence after marriage through one parent's line (Enke, 2019, QJE; Schulz et al., 2019, Science). [B]

Phylogenetic studies show that kinship systems respond to how people make a living. In Africa, the spread of cattle herding was linked to the loss of matrilineal descent (Holden & Mace, 2003, Proc. R. Soc. B). In Austronesian societies, residence with the wife's family was probably the ancestral pattern, not residence with the husband's (Jordan et al., 2009, Proc. R. Soc. B). [B]

D5.2 Key ancient DNA studies

Since about 2008, ancient DNA has let researchers rebuild actual family trees from burials. This is the most important new evidence for your chapter.

What the ancient DNA evidence adds up to

  1. Biological kinship mattered in prehistoric burial, but it was never the only rule. Unrelated people, adoptees and dependents appear repeatedly.

  2. Patrilocality dominates the European samples, but this may partly reflect which sites have been studied (monumental tombs, elite burials).

  3. Matrilineal and mixed systems existed (Fujia, Anatolia, Mokrin). There was no single “original” family form.

  4. Households included non-kin: servants, dependents, possibly enslaved people (Lech valley). This anticipates the Greek oikos and Roman familia.

Caution on "the earliest nuclear family": Eulau (about 4,600 years ago) is often called the oldest genetically confirmed nuclear family. Its authors themselves note that this does not make the nuclear family universal or the oldest institution. The Anatolian sites show nuclear-family relatives buried in the same houses about 10,000 years ago. Use the wording "the oldest direct genetic proof of a mother–father–children grave".

D6. Families in the First Civilizations

Across early states, three features recur: (1) the household as the basic unit of economy, law and religion; (2) authority held by a senior male, with real exceptions; (3) marriage as a contract between families about property and legitimate heirs. The details differ, and the differences are where the interesting material is.

D6.1 Mesopotamia

  • Law codes: Ur-Nammu (around 2100 BCE, the oldest surviving code) already regulates divorce and marriage payments. Hammurabi (reigned around 1792–1750 BCE) has dozens of family laws on marriage, divorce, adoption, inheritance and widows. [A] Standard translation: Roth, Law Collections from Mesopotamia and Asia Minor (1995).

  • Marriage payments: terhatum (bride-wealth) went from the groom's side to the bride's father. Šeriktum (dowry) went with the bride and remained hers, passing to her children. [A]

  • Values: the "house" (bītum) was an economic and ritual unit. Children, especially sons, were expected to care for parents and perform rites for dead ancestors (kispum). [A]

  • Women: generally subordinate in law, but some women had real economic roles. The nadītum women of Sippar managed property and business; Old Assyrian merchant wives ran trading operations (Stol, Women in the Ancient Near East, 2016). [A]

D6.2 Egypt

  • Women's legal standing was unusually strong for the ancient world: they could own and inherit property, make contracts, go to court and initiate divorce (Robins, Women in Ancient Egypt, 1993). [A] But "equal rights" overstates it: men still dominated office-holding, literacy and public life.

  • Marriage was a private, social act with no religious ceremony required. Property contracts are well attested from the Late Period onward. Polygyny was mostly royal or elite; ordinary households were mostly monogamous. [A]

  • Divine families: Osiris–Isis–Horus (the central mythic family) and the Theban triad of Amun–Mut–Khonsu. Religion modeled ideal family roles. [A] (The earlier draft's "Ra and Mut" is wrong.)

  • Household evidence: the workmen's village at Deir el-Medina gives unusually detailed records of daily family life, divorce and inheritance (Toivari-Viitala, 2001). [A]

D6.3 Greece (Classical Athens)

  • Oikos: a household of people, property, slaves and ancestral cult. The kyrios (male guardian) legally represented women and minors. [A]

  • Marriage: a betrothal (engyē) arranged between the kyrios and the groom, with a dowry (proix) that had to be returned on divorce. The father decided whether a newborn was accepted into the household. [A]

  • Epiklēros: a daughter with no brothers was married to a close male relative so the estate stayed in the family, which shows that the oikos mattered more than individual choice. [A]

  • Variation: Sparta differed sharply. Women had more property and freedom, and the state took over boys' education. Avoid writing "Greek family" as if it were one thing (Pomeroy, Families in Classical and Hellenistic Greece, 1997). [A]

D6.4 Rome

  • **Paterfamilias and patria potestas: the oldest living male held legal power over his descendants, including in theory the power of life and death. [A]* Saller (Patriarchy, Property and Death in the Roman Family, 1994) showed that demographics limited this in practice: many fathers died before their children reached adulthood. [B]*

  • *Familia: meant the whole household, including slaves, not just blood relatives. The English word "family" comes from this. [A]*

  • Marriage: by the late Republic, marriage sine manu (in which the wife stayed under her father's authority) was dominant, giving women more control over property. Augustus's marriage laws (18 BCE, 9 CE) penalized celibacy and childlessness among the elite: early state family policy (Treggiari, Roman Marriage, 1991). [A]

D6.5 China

  • Confucian family ethics: filial piety (xiao), hierarchy by age and sex, and ancestor worship. The family was the model for the state: ruler and subject mirrored father and son. [A]

  • Patrilineal lineages with shared surnames and exogamy rules; corporate lineages became important especially in late imperial South China (Freedman, Lineage Organization in Southeastern China, 1958). [A]

  • Nuance: Ebrey (The Inner Quarters, 1993) shows that Song-dynasty women had real agency in marriage and property, within a patriarchal frame. And Fujia (Section 5) shows that China's deep past was not always patrilineal. [A]

D6.6 South Asia

  • The joint family: brothers, their wives and children living together under shared property, an ideal that persists into the present. [A]

  • Normative texts like the Manusmṛti (early centuries CE) set out household duties, the stages of life (āśrama), and lineage (gotra) exogamy. These are prescriptions, not descriptions; how people actually lived varied. [B]

D7. Europe, 300–1800: How the “Western Family” Was Made

D7.1 The Church and the breakup of the clan

*Jack Goody, The Development of the Family and Marriage in Europe (1983): from late antiquity onward, the Western Church banned marriage between relatives (eventually as far as sixth cousins, scaled back at the Fourth Lateran Council in 1215), as well as marriage to a dead husband's brother (levirate), adoption as a means of inheritance, and polygyny, and it promoted consent-based marriage. Goody argued that one effect was that more property passed to the Church. [B] The motive he proposed is contested; the bans themselves* are well documented.

*Schulz, Bahrami-Rad, Beauchamp & Henrich (2019, Science): the longer a population was exposed to the medieval Western Church, the lower its rates of cousin marriage, and the more individualistic and "impersonally prosocial" its people are today. Expanded in Henrich, The WEIRDest People in the World (2020). [C]* The paper is highly influential, but historians and economists have challenged its causal claims and measures.

D7.2 The European marriage pattern (Hajnal line)

Hajnal (1965) identified a pattern west of a line from roughly St Petersburg to Trieste: late marriage (women in their mid-20s), many people never marrying, and newlyweds setting up their own household, often after years working as servants in other households. [A] This is a pre-industrial pattern, and economic historians argue that it may have helped make industrialization possible (de Moor & van Zanden, 2010). [C]

D7.3 Household size: Laslett and the Cambridge Group

Laslett and the Cambridge Group found that in English communities between 1564 and 1821, only about 10% of households included relatives beyond the nuclear family, about the same as in 1966. Large extended households were not the pre-industrial norm in England. [A] (Laslett, The World We Have Lost, 1965; Laslett & Wall, Household and Family in Past Time, 1972.)

Important caveat: this applies to northwestern Europe. Eastern and Southern Europe, and much of Asia, did have more complex joint and stem households (Hajnal, 1982; Mitterauer & Sieder, 1982; Todd, 2011). [A]

D8. Industrialization to Today

D8.1 What industrialization actually changed

  • Separating work from home: production moved out of the household, so the family became a unit of consumption and emotion rather than production. [A]

  • "Separate spheres" ideology: the male breadwinner and the female homemaker as a middle-class ideal (Davidoff & Hall, Family Fortunes, 1987). Working-class women and children kept working for wages. [A]

  • Kin did not disappear: in Preston in 1851, Anderson (1971) found that migrants relied more on co-resident relatives, not less. Hareven (Family Time and Industrial Time, 1982) showed families working together to recruit and support each other in the Amoskeag textile mills. [A]

  • Theory at the time: Parsons & Bales (1955) presented the "isolated nuclear family" as the functional fit for industrial society. Goode (World Revolution and Family Patterns, 1963) predicted global convergence toward the conjugal family. [B] Thornton (Reading History Sideways, 2005) criticized the assumption that every society moves along the same path ("developmental idealism").

D8.2 The 20th century

  • The 1950s breadwinner family was historically unusual: very young marriage, high marriage rates and a baby boom, not a timeless "tradition" (Coontz, The Way We Never Were, 1992; Marriage, a History, 2005). [A]

  • The "Second Demographic Transition" (Lesthaeghe & van de Kaa, 1986): rising individualism leads to later marriage, cohabitation, more divorce, fertility below replacement and births outside marriage. [B] This fits Western Europe best; East Asia follows a different path (low fertility but low rates of non-marital births).

  • Marriage loses its institutional grip: Cherlin (2004) describes the "deinstitutionalization" of marriage, moving from institutional to companionate to individualized marriage. [B]

  • Legal change: no-fault divorce; recognition of non-marital children; same-sex marriage (first in the Netherlands in 2001; in the US nationwide in 2015 through Obergefell). [A]

  • Chosen and diverse families: Weston, Families We Choose (1991); Stacey, Brave New Families (1990). [B]

Narrative opportunity

The history of the theories has its own arc: from 19th-century fantasies of stages (promiscuity, then matriarchy, then patriarchy) to 20th-century claims of universality, to today’s answer of flexibility. The question “what was the first family?” has itself changed shape. That could be a strong opening for Chapter 1.
